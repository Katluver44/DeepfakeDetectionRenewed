================================================================================
ELI5.txt  —  A plain-language explainer for the paper
"Low-Cost Geometric Directions in Frozen Speech Models: Where a Training-Free
 Deepfake-Hardness Signal Helps — and Where It Does Not"
================================================================================

Who this is for: a college freshman who knows what a mean, a standard
deviation, and a correlation are, and who has seen a picture of a neural
network. By the end you should understand every concept well enough to write
about this paper accurately.

How to read it: each numbered section gives (a) the plain idea with an everyday
analogy, then (b) "In this paper specifically:" with the actual numbers and how
the concept is used in THIS repository. Sections 1–24 cover the paper as
written; sections 25–32 cover the future work in the submission roadmap.

The one-sentence summary of the whole paper: inside a big pre-trained speech AI
there is a single hidden "real-vs-fake" direction; how SPREAD-OUT a fake-voice
system's outputs are along that direction predicts how hard that system is to
catch; that direction is different in every dataset (which is why detectors
don't transfer); and you can bolt the direction onto a detector for free to help
it — but only when the detector is weak enough to have room to improve.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART A — THE BUILDING BLOCKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. SELF-SUPERVISED SPEECH MODEL / WavLM
---------------------------------------
Plain idea: A "self-supervised" model is one that teaches itself by playing
fill-in-the-blank on huge amounts of raw data, with no human labels. Imagine
someone who listened to tens of thousands of hours of speech and constantly
tried to guess the masked-out chunk of each recording. To get good at that game
they had to build a rich internal sense of how speech "works" — sounds,
phonemes, speaker traits, acoustics. WavLM is exactly such a model for audio.

In this paper specifically: The backbone is WavLM-base, a ~95-million-parameter
(94.7M) model from Chen et al. 2022. It was pre-trained self-supervised on ~960
hours of speech. The paper treats this model as a fixed measuring instrument:
raw audio (16 kHz, 3-second clips) goes in, and internal representations come
out. Nothing about WavLM is retrained.

2. "FROZEN"
-----------
Plain idea: "Frozen" means you lock all the model's internal dials and never
change them. You only USE the model; you don't teach it anything new. Like using
a camera exactly as it ships from the factory instead of modifying its lens.

In this paper specifically: WavLM's ~95M parameters are frozen. Only a small
~13M-parameter "head" on top is ever trained (see §17). All the geometry the
paper studies is measured in the frozen representation, "before any decision
boundary is drawn." Freezing is what makes the method cheap (one GPU, no
retraining) and is central to the paper's identity as a small-scale/low-cost
method.

3. ENCODER LAYERS / WHY LAYER 12 / MEAN-POOLING OVER TIME
---------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea (layers): A deep network processes input in stages ("layers"), each
refining the previous one's output. Early layers in a speech model capture
low-level acoustic detail (loudness, pitch, microphone character); middle/later
layers capture more abstract linguistic content (phonemes, words, meaning).
Think of an assembly line where each station adds more interpretation.

Plain idea (mean-pooling over time): A 3-second clip is a sequence of many time
frames, each with its own vector. "Mean-pooling over time" just averages those
frames into ONE vector for the whole clip — like summarizing a paragraph by the
average of its sentence meanings. It throws away the moment-to-moment order but
gives one fixed-size fingerprint per clip.

Why layer 12: WavLM-base has 12 transformer layers. Layer 12 is the last/deepest
one, where representations are most linguistically abstract and most separated
between natural and synthetic speech. The paper reads all its geometry from this
layer.

In this paper specifically: For each utterance they take WavLM
hidden_states[12] (layer 12 of 12) and do `.mean(1)` — mean-pool over the time
axis — producing one 768-dimensional vector per clip. Every axis, centroid, and
projection in the paper lives in this "frozen WavLM-L12, mean-pooled" space. (A
planned experiment, §26, will sweep all layers 0–12 to show the effect RISES
with depth — evidence it's learned structure, not a microphone artifact.)

4. EMBEDDINGS, 768-DIMENSIONAL SPACE, AND WHAT "A DIRECTION / AXIS" MEANS
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea (embedding): An embedding is a list of numbers that represents an
object as a point in space. If I describe a person by (height, weight), that's a
2-D embedding and each person is a dot on a plane. Speech clips get a 768-number
embedding, so each clip is a dot in a 768-dimensional space. We can't picture
768 dimensions, but the math (distances, averages, angles) works exactly like it
does in 2-D or 3-D.

Plain idea (a direction / axis): In that space, a "direction" is an arrow — a
768-number vector — pointing some way. Any point can be "projected onto" the
arrow: you drop a perpendicular from the point to the arrow and read off how far
along you land. That single number is the point's "position along the axis."
Think of a ruler laid across a cloud of dots: each dot gets a reading.

In this paper specifically: Each utterance = a point in 768-D frozen WavLM-L12
space. The "natural-vs-synthetic axis" is one specific arrow in that space (§6).
Projecting an utterance onto it gives one number saying "how synthetic-ish this
clip looks to the encoder."


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART B — THE AXIS AND THE LAW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

5. THE NATURAL <-> SYNTHETIC AXIS: HOW IT'S COMPUTED
----------------------------------------------------
Plain idea: Take all the REAL (bona fide) clips; their embeddings form a cloud
with a center of mass (the "centroid" = the average point). Take all the FAKE
(spoof) clips; they have their own centroid. Draw the arrow from the fake center
to the real center (or real minus fake). That arrow is the "real-vs-fake ruler."
It's built purely from group averages — no trained classifier, just two centers
and the line between them. This is why it's called "unsupervised" in the sense of
"no detector/decision-boundary needed," even though it does use the bona/spoof
group labels.

Standardization: Before computing the arrow, each of the 768 coordinates is put
on a common scale (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation, computed
on the bona-fide clips). Analogy: if one feature is measured in millimeters and
another in miles, you first convert everyone to "number of standard deviations
from average" so no single coordinate dominates just because its raw units are
big. In the code: mu = mean of train bona, sd = std of train bona, then
z = (X - mu)/sd before taking centroid differences.

"Corpus-internal": The axis is estimated separately WITHIN each dataset
("corpus"), using only that dataset's own real and fake clips. It is never
imported from another dataset. This matters enormously because the axis turns
out to point a different way in every corpus (§10).

Leave-one-system-out (LOSO) and why: A "system" is one particular voice-faking
program (a specific TTS model). To measure how hard system A is, we must not let
system A's own clips help define the ruler used to judge system A — that would be
circular ("grading your own exam"). So when scoring system A, we build the axis
from the real clips plus every OTHER system's fakes, leaving A out. Repeat,
leaving each system out in turn. This guarantees a system never informs its own
score. (The code in i7_axis_fusion.py does the fold-level analog: the axis for
each held-out fold is built only from the OTHER folds' data.)

In this paper specifically: w = centroid(spoof) − centroid(bona) in standardized
frozen WavLM-L12 space, estimated corpus-internally and leave-one-system-out on
MLAAD's 61 TTS systems. It is deliberately NOT the detector's own decision
boundary — a point the paper stresses to defeat the "it's circular" objection
(§20).

6. sd_along (SPREAD ALONG THE AXIS) vs POSITION ALONG THE AXIS — AND WHY SPREAD
   IS "THE LAW"
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea: Project all of one system's fake clips onto the ruler. Now you have a
bunch of readings. You can summarize them two ways:
  - POSITION = the AVERAGE reading (where the system sits on the ruler, e.g. "how
    fake-looking on average"). This is the intuitive first guess: "the closer a
    system's fakes sit to the real end, the harder to catch."
  - SPREAD (sd_along) = the STANDARD DEVIATION of the readings (how strung-out
    the system's clips are along the ruler). A system with tight spread makes
    fakes that all look about equally fake; a system with big spread makes some
    fakes that look very fake and OTHERS that look almost genuine.

The paper's key finding — "the law" — is that SPREAD, not position, predicts
hardness. The mechanism: a system that spreads across the boundary produces some
utterances that look essentially genuine to the encoder, and THOSE are exactly
the ones that slip past the detector. Position alone (the obvious guess) does NOT
survive; it is in fact the form that FAILED in the pre-registered test (§18).
This is why the paper insists the result is not a triviality: the naive "closer =
harder" story loses, and a second-moment quantity (a standard deviation) wins.

In this paper specifically: sd_along = the standard deviation of a system's
per-utterance projections onto the corpus-internal axis w. Across MLAAD's 61
systems, higher sd_along => harder to detect (Spearman rho = 0.60). It is "the
only quantity, out of 83 tested, to survive a global false-discovery-rate
correction" (§9).

7. DETECTION HARDNESS: 1 − AUC, and AUC / ROC / EER / FALSE POSITIVE vs FALSE
   NEGATIVE / WHY EER
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea (a detector's score): A detector outputs a number ("logit") for each
clip — higher = "more likely fake." To make a decision you pick a threshold:
above it, call "fake"; below, call "real."

False positive vs false negative:
  - FALSE POSITIVE = calling a genuine clip "fake" (false alarm).
  - FALSE NEGATIVE = calling a fake clip "genuine" (a miss — the dangerous one
    for security). "False positive rate" (FPR) and "false negative rate" (FNR)
    move in opposite directions as you slide the threshold: catch more fakes and
    you also cry wolf more often.

ROC curve: Slide the threshold across all values and plot true-positive rate vs
false-positive rate. That traced curve is the ROC. A perfect detector hugs the
top-left corner; a coin-flip detector is the diagonal line.

AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve): the area under that curve, from 0.5 (useless) to
1.0 (perfect). Intuition: AUC = the probability that a randomly chosen fake gets
a higher "fake" score than a randomly chosen real clip. It's threshold-free.

Hardness = 1 − AUC: If AUC = 1 (easy, perfectly separable), hardness = 0. If
AUC = 0.5 (impossible, fully overlapping), hardness = 0.5. So "1 − AUC" is a
clean per-system difficulty score: high = hard to detect. Class overlap (fakes
sitting among reals) directly lowers AUC — which is why the "spread across the
boundary" mechanism (§6) connects so naturally to hardness.

EER (Equal Error Rate): the single threshold where FPR = FNR (false alarms =
misses). The value they share is the EER. Lower is better; 0.5 (50%) is
chance. People use EER because it collapses the whole trade-off into one
threshold-independent number that doesn't require picking an operating point, and
it's the standard metric in the anti-spoofing field.

In this paper specifically: Per-system hardness = 1 − AUC of the detector's
scores for that system's fakes against a shared pool of genuine clips. Fusion
gains are reported as EER changes (e.g., 0.144 -> 0.114 on the weak detector).
The code's `eer_of()` finds the threshold where |FPR − FNR| is smallest and
returns their average.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART C — STATISTICS AND WHY THE RESULT IS TRUSTWORTHY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

8. SPEARMAN rho, PERMUTATION p-VALUE, LOSO R^2
----------------------------------------------
Spearman rho (rank correlation): Plain idea: ordinary (Pearson) correlation
asks "do the actual values move together in a straight line?" Spearman asks the
gentler question "do the RANKINGS agree?" — rank the systems by sd_along, rank
them by hardness, and see if the two orderings match. rho = +1 means identical
ordering, 0 means unrelated, −1 means reversed. It's robust to outliers and
non-straight relationships, which is why it's used for the "law."

Permutation p-value: Plain idea: "Could I have gotten a correlation this strong
by pure luck?" To find out, shuffle the hardness labels randomly thousands of
times, breaking any real link, and recompute the correlation each time. If almost
none of these shuffled (fake) datasets reach the correlation you actually
observed, your result is unlikely to be coincidence. The p-value is the fraction
of shuffles that matched or beat your real number. Small p = unlikely to be luck.
It makes no assumptions about the data's shape — you build the "luck distribution"
by brute force.

LOSO R^2 (leave-one-system-out R-squared): R^2 ("coefficient of determination")
is "what fraction of the variation in hardness can my predictor explain?" 0 =
nothing, 1 = everything. "LOSO" means you fit the predictor while holding out
each system, then test on the held-out one — an honest, non-cheating estimate of
how well sd_along predicts a NEW system's hardness, not just systems it was fit
on.

In this paper specifically: On MLAAD's 61 systems, sd_along vs hardness gives
Spearman rho = 0.60 (p < 10^-6), leave-one-system-out R^2 = 0.277 (permutation
p = 2.5 x 10^-4), bootstrap rho 95% CI [0.41, 0.73]. Note: an earlier reported
R^2 of 0.316 was wrong — that was a 3-feature model mislabeled as sd_along-alone;
the audit corrected the single-feature value to 0.277.

9. "THE LAW" AND "SURVIVES GLOBAL BH-FDR OUT OF 83 TESTS" (MULTIPLE COMPARISONS)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea (the multiple-comparisons problem): If you run ONE test, a p < 0.05
"false alarm by luck" happens 5% of the time. But if you run 83 tests, you'd
EXPECT about 4 of them to look "significant" by pure chance even if nothing is
real. So finding a small p-value among many tests is much weaker evidence than
finding it in a single planned test. Analogy: buy 83 lottery tickets and one
wins — that's not proof you're psychic.

Benjamini–Hochberg False Discovery Rate (BH-FDR): a standard procedure that
corrects for this. It sorts all the p-values and applies a sliding, stricter bar
so that among everything you end up calling "significant," only a small
controlled fraction are expected to be false. "Global BH-FDR across 83 tests"
means the correction was applied to EVERYTHING tested in the whole project at
once — the harshest possible bookkeeping.

In this paper specifically: Of all 83 quantities tested across the study,
sd_along -> hardness is the ONLY one that survives global BH-FDR (audit reports
q < 0.001). Everything else — including a whole feature battery whose best was
q = 0.078 — fails the global bar. That is why the paper elevates this one result
to "the law" and treats the rest as exploratory. Caveat surfaced by the audit:
the "83" count will change as new experiments are added, so the number should be
kept in sync with the final ledger.

10. THE AXIS ROTATES ACROSS CORPORA: COSINE SIMILARITY, CHANCE FLOOR, SPLIT-HALF
    RELIABILITY, "POINTING BACKWARDS"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Cosine similarity: Plain idea: a number from −1 to +1 measuring whether two
arrows point the same way, ignoring their lengths. +1 = identical direction,
0 = perpendicular (unrelated), −1 = opposite directions. It's just the cosine of
the angle between them.

Chance floor in 768 dimensions: Plain idea: in very high-dimensional space, two
arrows drawn at random are almost always nearly perpendicular — their cosine sits
near 0. (This is a real geometric fact: random unit vectors in n dimensions have
expected |cosine| about 1/sqrt(n).) So "cosine near 0" between two axes means
"no more aligned than two random arrows" — i.e., unrelated. In 768-D this chance
floor is about 0.03.

Split-half reliability: Plain idea: to check that a measurement is trustworthy
(not noise), split the data in half, compute the thing on each half, and see if
the two halves agree. If splitting a corpus and estimating its axis twice gives
nearly the same arrow, the axis is measured cleanly, not by luck.

Why a transferred axis can point BACKWARDS: If corpus A's axis and corpus B's
axis have a NEGATIVE cosine, then using A's ruler on B doesn't just fail
randomly — it systematically ranks things in reverse. The "real end" of A's ruler
points toward B's "fake" region. That's worse than useless.

In this paper specifically: Within one corpus the axis is nearly noise-free
(split-half cosine ~0.93, up to 0.98). But the MLAAD axis and the ASVspoof-2019
axis have cosine about −0.08 (slightly negative!), far below the within-corpus
0.93 and inside the ~0.03 chance band. MLAAD-vs-ASVspoof2021 is even more
negative (−0.14 to −0.21). Conclusion: each dataset has its OWN real-vs-fake
direction; a transferred axis is uninformative or backwards. This is the paper's
"geometric reason detectors fail to transfer across domains." (An earlier draft
wrongly reported this cosine as +0.36 — a number that was actually a
within-MLAAD quantity; the audit caught and reversed it.)

11. RELIABILITY CEILING 0.86 / DISATTENUATION
---------------------------------------------
Plain idea: No measurement is perfectly repeatable — the hardness score itself
has noise. If a target you're trying to predict is only, say, 86% self-consistent
when re-measured, then no predictor on Earth can explain more than that 86%; the
remaining 14% is just measurement noise, not real signal. That ceiling is set by
the hardness metric's own reliability.

Disattenuation: Plain idea: because your yardstick is noisy, the correlation you
observe is an UNDER-estimate of the true underlying relationship. "Disattenuating"
(correcting for attenuation) divides out the known noise to estimate what the
correlation WOULD be if you could measure perfectly. It's the flip side of the
"reliability ceiling."

In this paper specifically: The hardness metric's split-half reliability is 0.86,
so the R^2 ceiling is ~0.86. sd_along's R^2 of 0.277 is therefore about 0.277 /
0.86 ~ 32% of the EXPLAINABLE variance — "a partial but robust account," not a
full one. (Watch-out flagged by the roadmap: the paper's Setup section currently
also says "split-half reliability 0.76," which conflicts with the audit's 0.86 —
one of these is a typo/definition mismatch to be fixed. The audit value is 0.86.)
The "attenuation signature of a real effect": rho rises 0.60 -> 0.64 -> 0.69 as
you require >=8, >=12, >=16 utterances per system, i.e., as measurement noise
falls — exactly what a true effect (not an artifact) does.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART D — FUSION, FOLDS, AND HEADROOM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

12. AXIS FUSION: fused = z(logit) + lambda * z(axis)
----------------------------------------------------
Plain idea: You have two opinions about each clip: the detector's score (logit)
and the axis projection. Fusion just adds them into one combined score — but
first you put both on the same scale, then weight the axis by a knob lambda.

z-scoring, and why train-fold-only stats matter:
  - z-score: convert a number to "how many standard deviations above/below its
    mean," i.e. z = (value − mean)/SD. Now the detector's logits and the axis
    projections live on a common, unit-free scale so you can sensibly add them.
  - Train-fold-only stats: the mean and SD used for z-scoring (and the choice of
    lambda) must be computed ONLY on the training data, never on the data you
    evaluate on. Otherwise you've secretly peeked at the answers — the evaluation
    numbers would be optimistic and dishonest ("leakage," §14). In the code, mu,
    sd, lm, ls, pm, ps are all computed on `tr_mask` (the train fold) only.

lambda (the trust knob): lambda controls how much you trust the axis relative to
the detector. lambda = 0 means "ignore the axis, use the detector alone." Large
lambda means "lean heavily on the axis." It's the dial between the two opinions.

The grid {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.5, 2, 3}: rather than solve for the best
lambda analytically, they just try a fixed menu of values and keep whichever
works best.

How lambda is chosen — on the TRAINING fold, never on eval: for each fold, they
pick the lambda that maximizes AUC on that fold's TRAINING data, then apply it to
the held-out test data. Choosing lambda by looking at the test data would be
cheating. In the code: `best = max(LAMS, key=lambda L: roc_auc_score(ytr,
ztr_l + L*ztr_p))` — note `ytr`, the train labels.

Why capping the grid at 2.0 broke the ITW experiment: On the In-the-Wild dataset,
the training folds kept selecting the LARGEST allowed lambda (the grid maxed at
2.0 and 2.0 was chosen in all folds) — a tell-tale sign the optimum was pinned at
the boundary and the true best weight wanted to be even higher (or that the axis
should dominate the detector entirely). Because the grid was capped too low, the
procedure couldn't express "trust the axis a lot more than the weak detector,"
and the fusion result was misleading. The rectified analysis (ITW_FUSION_
REANALYSIS) found that on ITW, fusing actually HURTS relative to the axis alone
(fusion EER 0.306 vs axis-alone 0.301) — the opposite of the original claim. A
capped grid plus correlated errors (§16) is why the ITW experiment went wrong.

In this paper specifically: The MLAAD in-domain fusion (0.272 -> 0.163, axis
alone 0.187) and the weak-detector ASVspoof fusion (0.144 -> 0.114) are the clean
fusion wins; the ITW "win" was a mislabeling artifact that the audit reversed.

13. FOLDS / CROSS-VALIDATION / SPEAKER- & SYSTEM-DISJOINT SPLITS
---------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea (cross-validation & folds): To test honestly, split your data into
K equal parts ("folds"). Train on K−1 of them, test on the held-out one, rotate
so each fold gets a turn as the test set, average the results. This reuses data
efficiently while never testing on what you trained on.

Speaker-disjoint / system-disjoint splits: A plain split can accidentally put the
same speaker (or the same TTS system) in both train and test. Then the model can
"win" just by recognizing a familiar voice or a familiar generator, not by
learning the real task. "Speaker-disjoint" means no speaker appears in both
train and test; "system-disjoint" means no synthesis system does. This forces the
evaluation to measure generalization to NEW voices/systems.

In this paper specifically: The fusion experiment (i7) uses 5-fold, SYSTEM-
disjoint folds (spoof systems partitioned across folds; bona split into the same
folds), with axis, z-stats, and lambda all fit on train folds only. The ITW work
requires SPEAKER-disjoint folds — and that's where the trouble was (§14).

14. LEAKAGE — THE ITW SPEAKER-LEAKAGE STORY (~3-4 EER POINTS)
------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea (leakage): "Leakage" is any way information from the test set sneaks
into training, making results look better than they truly are. It's the silent
killer of ML papers.

The ITW story: In the In-the-Wild fusion pipeline, genuine ("bona") clips were
originally split into folds at RANDOM, independent of speaker. Because the same
speaker's clips could then land in both the axis-training fold and the test fold,
the model effectively saw the test speakers during training — leakage. When the
folds were made strictly speaker-disjoint (each speaker's clips confined to one
fold), the EER went UP because the artificial boost disappeared: e.g., the
supervised-LDA-fusion number rose from 0.161 (leaky) to 0.193 (strict) — about
3.3 EER points of the apparent performance were pure leakage. Roughly 3-4 EER
points of the original ITW claim evaporated once leakage was closed.

In this paper specifically: This is a "what does NOT work / what we corrected"
result. It does not touch the core law or the clean MLAAD fusion; it's a honesty
correction to the domain-shift narrative, and the word "zero-cost" is banned for
any variant that used labels.

15. HEADROOM: WHY FUSION HELPS WEAK DETECTORS AND NOT NEAR-CEILING ONES
----------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea: "Headroom" is how much room a detector has left to improve. A student
scoring 55% has lots of room to gain from a tutor; a student already scoring 99%
has almost none — a tutor can't add much. The free axis signal is like that
tutor: it helps a weak detector a lot and a near-perfect detector essentially not
at all, because there's nothing left to fix.

In this paper specifically: A deliberately WEAK WavLM-GAT trained on only 500
utterances had EER 0.144; axis fusion cut it to 0.114 (ΔEER = −0.030, 95% CI
[−0.046, −0.013], improving all 5 folds — a ~21% relative reduction). The
identical procedure on a STRONG, near-ceiling WavLM-GAT (EER ~0.071–0.078)
produced no change beyond noise (ΔEER −0.0004 to +0.004, p ~ 0.56–0.80). The
roadmap's X6 turns this two-point anecdote into a full "headroom curve" over
7 training budgets (§29).

16. CORRELATED ERRORS: WHY FUSING TWO CORRELATED SCORES CAN HURT
---------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea: Combining two opinions helps most when they make DIFFERENT mistakes —
their errors cancel. If two judges always get fooled by the same clips (their
errors are correlated), averaging them adds nothing and can even amplify a shared
blind spot. Ensembling folklore says "combining always helps," but that's only
true when the components are diverse.

In this paper specifically: On ITW the detector's errors and the axis's errors
are correlated and both partly driven by recording-channel differences (§21), so
fusing them doesn't cancel mistakes — it hurts (fusion 0.306 vs axis-alone
0.301). This is precisely why the paper frames fusion's value as CONDITIONAL, not
universal, and reports the "does not help" regime with the same care as the wins.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART E — THE DETECTORS, PRE-REGISTRATION, AND CONFOUNDS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

17. THE DETECTORS: WavLM-GAT, AASIST, mini_goat vs robust_goat, FINE-TUNED vs
    ZERO-SHOT
----------------------------------------------------------------------------
What a GAT is (roughly): A Graph Attention Network treats data as a graph — nodes
connected by edges — and lets each node "pay attention to" (weight) its neighbors
when updating itself. "Attention" = learned importance weights: some neighbors
matter more. Here the nodes are phonemes (speech-sound units), so the model
compares phoneme-level features and learns which phoneme relationships betray a
fake. (Velickovic et al. 2018 introduced GATs.)

WavLM-GAT (the primary detector): A frozen WavLM encoder feeds a small trainable
head: adaptive phoneme pooling -> a 3-layer, 6-head graph-attention network with
skip connections -> a 2-layer BiLSTM -> mean-pool over phonemes + L2-normalize ->
a linear head -> one spoof logit. Frozen encoder ~95M params; trainable head
~13M. Architecture follows Zhang et al. 2025.

AASIST: A different, well-known anti-spoofing architecture (Jung et al. 2022),
also graph-attention-based but spectro-temporal, NOT built on WavLM. The paper
uses it to show the law is not a WavLM/GAT quirk — the rank-order law replicates
on AASIST (rho = 0.35, p = 0.006), which defeats the "circular / self-referential"
objection because AASIST doesn't share WavLM's geometry.

mini_goat vs robust_goat: These are two training budgets of the same WavLM-GAT.
"mini_goat" = the deliberately WEAK detector (~500 utterances) with headroom;
"robust_goat" = the STRONG, near-ceiling detector. Fusion helps mini_goat and not
robust_goat — that's the headroom story (§15).

Fine-tuned vs zero-shot: "Fine-tuned" = the head was trained on the target task's
data. "Zero-shot" (ZS) = used as-is with no task-specific training. E.g.,
"AASIST-FT" is fine-tuned on MLAAD; "AASIST-ZS" is applied cold. Fine-tuning
usually helps in-domain but doesn't cure the cross-domain transfer failure the
paper diagnoses.

18. PRE-REGISTRATION: FROZEN SPEC, EXTERNAL TIMESTAMP, ONE-SHOT ACCEPTANCE, AND
    WHY THE FAILED TEST CAN'T BE RETRIED
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea: Pre-registration means writing down EXACTLY what you will test, how,
and what counts as success — BEFORE you look at the answer — and locking it so
you can't quietly change the rules after seeing results. It's the scientific
equivalent of calling your pool shot before taking it. It stops "p-hacking" (the
temptation to keep trying variations until something looks significant).

The pieces:
  - Frozen spec: a document (here, PREDICTOR_SPEC.md) fixing the single predictor
    (P3), the dataset, the statistic (Spearman rho), the direction (one-sided,
    rho > 0), the exact permutation-test settings (200,000 permutations, seed 7),
    and the minimum-utterances rule (>=20/attack) — with NO results in it.
  - External timestamp: to prove you didn't peek first, you commit the spec and
    push a git tag to a public server (GitHub) BEFORE scoring. The push time is an
    outside witness. (This fixed a flaw in the earlier attempt, where the "pre-
    registration" file was written by the same script only 63 seconds before
    scoring — no real outside commitment.)
  - One-shot acceptance criterion: success is defined in advance as a single
    binary rule — here, one-sided permutation p < 0.05. There is deliberately no
    "promising but not significant" middle category.
  - Why a failed test can't be retried: the whole point is that you get exactly
    ONE swing on that held-out data with that one predictor. If it fails, trying
    P1/P2/P4, another detector, another subset, or another dataset and reporting
    the best would destroy the guarantee — it converts an honest one-shot test
    back into fishing. So a fail must be reported as a fail, full stop.

In this paper specifically: A pre-registered, git-tag-timestamped test of the
axis-POSITION predictor (P3) on genuinely held-out ASVspoof2021-DF was run and
FAILED honestly: rho = +0.33, p = 0.135, n = 13 attacks (needed rho >= ~0.47 at
that tiny sample). The failure matters because (a) it shows the naive POSITION
form of the intuition does not confirm out-of-sample, while the SPREAD law
(sd_along) stands independently, and (b) reporting it strengthens the paper at an
audit-culture venue. The roadmap's X2 re-runs this at real statistical power
(n >= 40 systems, where rho ~ 0.3 suffices) as a new, separately pre-registered
test.

19. RELIABILITY / EXPLORATORY-vs-CONFIRMED / THE AUDIT
------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea: A "red-team audit" is having an independent skeptic recompute every
number from the raw data and try to break each claim. Findings get sorted into
"proven," "suggestive but not proven," and "wrong — fix it." Claims you set up in
advance and confirm are "confirmatory"; things you noticed after looking at the
data are "exploratory" and must be labeled as such (they're hypothesis-
GENERATING, not hypothesis-TESTING).

In this paper specifically: An independent audit recomputed all numbers from
cached artifacts and returned a tally of 8 verified, 6 mislabeled, 5 misleading,
2 contradicted claims — each corrected or removed. Examples corrected: the
cross-corpus cosine (+0.36 -> negative), the R^2 (0.316 -> 0.277), a "small
residual causal effect" dropped because its sign flipped across seeds, and
"13 interventions" -> the 22 conditions actually run. The paper reports the audit
in-line and keeps confirmed and exploratory results separate.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART F — MECHANISM, ADAPTATION, AND CONFOUNDS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

20. DIRECTIONALITY, NOT CIRCULARITY (why the law isn't a tautology)
-------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea: The worry: hardness is measured with a detector built on the SAME
frozen encoder that supplies the axis, so maybe "spread along the axis" is just
"spread along the detector's own decision line" — which would make the law true
by definition (circular). The paper answers this three ways: (a) the axis is a
two-centroid unsupervised direction, not the detector's boundary; (b) the naive
POSITION form failed while SPREAD survived — a tautology would not be so picky;
(c) the rank-order law replicates on AASIST, a detector that does NOT use WavLM's
geometry. Plus a causal probe: scrambling the top discriminative directions hurts
detection (ΔAUC −0.032) while scrambling an equal-size orthogonal residual does
not (+0.004) — showing the SPECIFIC direction carries the signal, not just
magnitude. (The paper is careful: only n = 3 seeds, so this is "trustworthy in
sign, not a significance claim.")

21. CHANNEL / RECORDING CONFOUND: vmean0 / LAYER-0 PROXIES, PARTIALLING OUT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea (channel): "Channel" = everything about HOW a clip was recorded and
transmitted, separate from WHAT was said or whether it's fake: the microphone,
room reverberation, background noise, the codec (MP3/Opus/etc.) and its bitrate,
sampling rate, telephone-line compression. Two clips of identical content can
look different to the encoder purely because of channel.

The confound worry: if real and fake clips in a corpus happen to differ in
channel (e.g., all fakes were encoded through the same vocoder pipeline), then a
"real-vs-fake" axis might really be sensing the MICROPHONE, not the synthesis.
The rotation across corpora (§10) could then be just different recording setups,
not different synthesis semantics.

vmean0 / layer-0 features as channel proxies: WavLM's layer 0 (and low-level
"velocity"/RMS-energy statistics) mostly capture acoustic/channel detail, not
linguistic content. So they serve as stand-ins ("proxies") for channel. If the
layer-12 law survives even after you remove ("partial out") what these channel
proxies explain, the law is probably about synthesis, not the microphone.

Partialling out: Plain idea: statistically "subtract off" the part of a
relationship explained by a nuisance variable, and see what's left. If sd_along
still predicts hardness after partialling out channel proxies, channel isn't
doing the work. (The paper does partial out n_utts, RMS, language, etc., and the
law survives; it candidly notes it "cannot fully exclude" that some of the axis
ROTATION reflects channel — which is exactly what roadmap experiments X4 and X5
are designed to settle.)

In this paper specifically: The paper concedes channel as an open question for
the rotation result, and the roadmap adds a layer-wise profile (X4) and a
channel-perturbation test (X5) to resolve whether rotation is channel or
synthesis semantics.

22. SHRINKAGE LDA (SUPERVISED AXIS) vs UNSUPERVISED CENTROID AXIS; "SUPERVISED
    LIGHTWEIGHT ADAPTATION" vs "ZERO-COST"
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis): Plain idea: a classic supervised way to find
the single direction that BEST separates two labeled groups — it accounts not
just for where the two centers are but for the spread/shape of each cloud, tilting
the separating line to maximize class separation. It needs labels.

Shrinkage (Ledoit-Wolf): In high dimensions (768!) with limited data, LDA's
covariance estimate is unstable. "Shrinkage" pulls that estimate toward a simple,
well-behaved default by an automatically chosen amount — a regularization trick
(Ledoit & Wolf 2004) that keeps the direction sensible. In code:
LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(solver="lsqr", shrinkage="auto").

Unsupervised centroid axis vs supervised LDA axis:
  - Centroid axis (the paper's main "axis"): just spoof-center minus bona-center.
    Simple, needs group labels only to form the two centers; "zero-cost" in the
    sense of no trained model.
  - Shrinkage-LDA axis: a fitted, supervised direction; more powerful but it
    consumes labels and fitting, so it is NOT "zero-cost."

"Supervised lightweight adaptation" vs "zero-cost": The audit found that the big
domain-shift "zero-cost fusion" gains were really coming from a supervised LDA
probe fitted on the target corpus's labels — which is lightweight ADAPTATION using
labels, not a free lunch. Honest framing: an unsupervised centroid axis is
zero-cost and helps in-domain (MLAAD) and even beats a fine-tuned detector on ITW;
but the label-using LDA gains are "supervised lightweight adaptation," and the
word "zero-cost" must not be used for them.

In this paper specifically: The confirmed clean fusion (MLAAD 0.272 -> 0.163,
weak-ASVspoof 0.144 -> 0.114) uses the UNSUPERVISED centroid axis. The
shrinkage-LDA axis is P3 in the pre-registered test and appears in the corrected
ITW adaptation story.

PART G — ANY OTHER AMBIGUOUS CONCEPTS WORTH NAILING DOWN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

23. BONA FIDE vs SPOOF; "SYSTEM" / "ATTACK"; TTS vs VC
------------------------------------------------------
- Bona fide = genuine human speech. Spoof = synthetic/fake speech. These are the
  two classes throughout.
- A "system" or "attack" = one specific speech-generation method (e.g., a
  particular TTS model, or ASVspoof's coded attacks A07–A19). Per-system hardness
  = how hard THAT generator is to catch.
- TTS (text-to-speech) = generate speech from written text (no human source
  voice for the words). VC (voice conversion) = take a REAL person's recording and
  convert it to sound like a different speaker, keeping the original content and
  prosody. The paper's law is a TTS phenomenon; on VC, all predictors give
  rho ~ 0 (§24), because converted speech inherits its content from real source
  speech, so its spread structure is different. (Roadmap X9 makes this a supported
  finding rather than an unbacked assertion.)

24. THE "83 TESTS" LEDGER AND FLOPs / COMPUTE BUDGET
----------------------------------------------------
- The "83-test ledger" is the running list of every statistical test in the
  project, used for the global BH-FDR bookkeeping (§9). Keeping this honest count
  is itself part of the paper's contribution ("method hygiene as a result").
- FLOPs (floating-point operations) = a hardware-neutral measure of how much
  computation a run costs. The paper stresses its LARGEST single training run is
  ~10^16 FLOPs, far under a 10^20 budget — i.e., this is genuinely small-scale
  (one GPU), which is its whole framing ("insight per FLOP," fit for a
  methods-at-small-scale venue).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART H — FUTURE / ROADMAP CONCEPTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

25. BACKBONE GENERALITY (X3)
----------------------------
Plain idea: Right now everything uses ONE self-supervised model (WavLM). "Backbone
generality" asks: is the law a property of WavLM specifically, or of
self-supervised speech geometry in general? To find out, re-embed the same clips
with other frozen backbones — wav2vec 2.0 and HuBERT — and re-check the law. If it
holds across three different SSL training objectives, it's a general property, not
a WavLM quirk. (This directly kills the paper's own stated limitation.)

26. LAYER SWEEP (X4)
--------------------
Plain idea: Instead of only layer 12, compute the law at EVERY layer 0–12. The
expected picture: near-chance at layer 0 (low-level acoustics) rising to a peak at
the deep layers. A rising profile is the cleanest possible evidence that the
signal is LEARNED linguistic structure, not a recording artifact — because a pure
channel artifact would show up strongest in the early (acoustic) layers, not the
deep ones. Paired with partialling out layer-0 channel proxies (§21).

27. CHANNEL-PERTURBATION ROTATION TEST (X5)
-------------------------------------------
Plain idea: To decide whether the axis rotation across corpora (§10) is about
recording CHANNEL or about synthesis SEMANTICS, take one corpus and deliberately
mangle its channel (MP3/Opus at two bitrates, add reverb, add noise, band-limit),
re-embed, and re-estimate the axis. If heavy channel abuse barely rotates the axis
(cosine stays well above 0.5) while merely switching corpora rotates it to ~0,
then rotation is driven by synthesis/content, not channel. If channel alone
reproduces the rotation, the honest story changes. Either outcome is publishable.

28. HEADROOM CURVE (X6)
-----------------------
Plain idea: Upgrade the two-point "weak vs strong" anecdote (§15) into a full
curve. Train detectors at seven budgets {125, 250, 500, 1k, 2k, 4k, full} with
2–3 seeds, apply the identical free axis fusion to each, and plot the fusion gain
(ΔEER) against the detector's own strength. The prediction: a smooth,
monotone relationship — big gains for weak detectors shrinking to zero at the
ceiling — giving a practitioner a rule for "when is the free lever worth it?"

29. AXIS-GUIDED EVASION ATTACK / REJECTION-SAMPLING THREAT MODEL (X8)
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea: Flip the law into an attack. Threat model: an attacker has a TTS
system and a copy of the public frozen WavLM, but NO access to the defender's
detector. Rejection sampling = generate many candidate fakes, score each by its
axis projection (how "genuine-looking"), and KEEP only the most bona-like fraction
(top 50% / 25% / 10%), throwing the rest away. Because spread-across-the-boundary
clips are the ones that evade (§6), selecting the genuine-looking tail should
raise the detector's miss rate — a zero-knowledge, detector-blind evasion lever.
Then test a countermeasure (axis-aware scoring) and check the attack transfers
across detectors (WavLM-GAT and AASIST). Note: this is SELECTION among generated
clips, not modifying the audio waveform — the framing must be precise. Compare at
matched sample size (selection shrinks n) against a random-selection null.

30. TTS vs VC AND SPREAD INHERITANCE (X9)
-----------------------------------------
Plain idea: Split every corpus's systems into TTS vs VC and report the law
separately for each, regenerating the currently-unbacked "rho ~ 0 on VC" claim
from actual artifacts. Mechanism probe ("spread inheritance"): for VC systems,
does the SOURCE speech's spread predict hardness? If so, VC fakes inherit their
spread structure from the real human recording they were converted from — a new
mechanism paragraph and the reason the TTS law doesn't apply to VC.

31. POOLED META-ANALYSIS / STOUFFER / RELIABILITY-CORRECTED (X10)
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Plain idea (meta-analysis): Combine evidence across many datasets into one
overall estimate. Pool the per-system (sd_along, hardness) pairs from all corpora
(MLAAD-en 61 + multilingual + ASVspoof19/21 + ...), z-scoring WITHIN each corpus
first (so a corpus's baseline channel differences can't masquerade as the law).

Stouffer's method: a standard way to combine several independent one-sided
p-values into a single overall p-value by converting each to a z-score, summing,
and re-normalizing. Caveat: corpora sharing a detector are not fully independent,
so the combined p must be labeled exploratory.

Reliability-corrected / disattenuated (see §11): report the disattenuated rho
using the 0.86 hardness reliability, which makes the "captures ~32% of explainable
variance" claim rigorous. Show a per-corpus forest plot (each corpus's rho with
its confidence interval, plus the pooled estimate) so genuine between-corpus
differences aren't hidden by the pooling.

32. CLUSTER BOOTSTRAP
---------------------
Plain idea (bootstrap): Estimate uncertainty by resampling your data WITH
replacement many times and recomputing the statistic each time; the spread of
those recomputations gives a confidence interval — no formula needed.

Cluster bootstrap: When data come in natural GROUPS (all utterances of one
speaker, or one system), plain per-utterance resampling pretends you have more
independent data points than you really do, giving falsely tight intervals. A
CLUSTER bootstrap resamples whole GROUPS (speakers/systems) instead of individual
utterances, respecting the dependence and giving honest, wider confidence
intervals. Used to size the fusion and headroom-curve error bars correctly.
